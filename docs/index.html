QUESTION 1

Where is growth coming from, and where should sales and promotion effort go?

Why it matters

Revenue more than doubled in a year. Knowing whether that came from new accounts or existing ones, and from which markets and product lines, shows where extra sales and promotion effort will pay off. It also shows where growth is fragile.

Approach

  • Compared Jan–Apr 1997 with Jan–Apr 1998. These are the only months observed in both years: the data starts in July 1996 and ends on 6 May 1998, so calendar years would mislead.
  • Revenue = unit price × quantity × (1 − discount), using the price actually charged on each order line.
  • Split the revenue change three ways: by type of customer (existing, new, won back, stopped buying), by customer country, and by product category and product, comparing each category with the company's overall growth.

Main finding

Revenue grew 121% ($191.3K to $422.3K). Existing customers ordering more often drove it: they added a net $189.2K, or 82% of the increase. Orders per customer rose from 1.9 to 3.2, but the average order grew only 6%. The nine new customers added just $25.2K.

The USA, Germany and Brazil produced 57% of the growth. France was flat (+5% on a $17.1K base), and Denmark, Canada, Mexico and Finland shrank. Seafood, Beverages and Dairy Products grew faster than the company. Grains/Cereals, Confections and Condiments lagged well behind it, with Grains/Cereals growing only 32%.

The biggest single gainer was Côte de Blaye (+$38.2K). Close behind was Thüringer Rostbratwurst (+$23.5K), which has since been discontinued.

Business implication

  • Keep deepening existing accounts, while still winning new ones. Existing customers ordering more often drove most of the growth, so regular reorder check-ins with established accounts should stay a priority, especially in the USA, Germany and Brazil. Question 3 shows which accounts to call first. New and won-back customers still added $66.8K, though, so prospecting for new customers should continue alongside.
  • Diagnose the stalled markets. France is flat and Denmark, Canada, Mexico and Finland are shrinking. Question 3 finds several French customers have gone quiet.
  • Aim promotions at the lagging categories, and expand the fast-growing ones. Promotions can lift Grains/Cereals, Confections and Condiments, where demand isn't keeping pace. Seafood, Beverages and Dairy Products show strong demand, so adding new products in these categories could capture more of it.
  • Find out why Thüringer Rostbratwurst was discontinued. It was one of the biggest gainers (+$23.5K). Unless Northwind is deliberately phasing out Meat/Poultry, it should source the product or an equivalent from another supplier so this demand isn't lost. Question 2 shows Meat/Poultry has almost no active products left.

QUESTION 2

Which suppliers do we depend on most, and where can our volume win better prices?

Why it matters

Northwind resells products from 29 suppliers. Purchasing terms are one of the few levers that improve margin without raising prices. Where Northwind is a large customer and could switch, it can ask for volume discounts. Where it can't easily switch, it needs to protect the relationship instead.

Approach

  • Used the last 12 complete months (May 1997–Apr 1998) as current annual volume.
  • The data has no purchase costs, so Northwind's sales of each supplier's products are a proxy for how much it buys from them. Discontinued products are excluded from this volume because they won't be bought again.
  • Measured how replaceable each supplier is by its share of its category's sales (weighted when it supplies several categories), plus the number of other suppliers with active products there.
  • Rule: among the top 10 suppliers by volume, a category share under 40% means negotiate, and 40% or more means protect. No top-10 supplier falls between 28% and 49%, so the result doesn't hinge on the exact cut-off.

Main finding

The top 10 suppliers account for 63% of ongoing sales. Six of them (Pavlova, Ltd., Forêts d'érables, Grandma Kelly's Homestead, Formaggi Fortini s.r.l., Norske Meierier and Specialty Biscuits, Ltd.) together sell $216.0K a year through Northwind (30% of ongoing sales). None holds more than 28% of its category, so Northwind could credibly move volume to a competitor. These are the best candidates for volume discounts.

The other four (Aux joyeux ecclésiastiques, Gai pâturage, G'day, Mate and Pasta Buttini s.r.l.) each hold 49%–57% of their category, worth $244.2K a year. Customers concentrate their spending on these suppliers' products, so pushing hard on price risks those sales.

The biggest supply risk is Meat/Poultry: 88% of the last 12 months' $106.5K in Meat/Poultry sales came from products that are now discontinued. Only Ma Maison (two products) still supplies active meat products.

Business implication

  • Open volume-discount talks with the six replaceable suppliers, starting with the largest (Pavlova, Ltd. and Forêts d'érables). Their products bring in $216.0K of sales a year, so every 1% off the purchase price saves a little under $2.2K a year. Purchase costs are lower than these sales figures.
  • Protect the four dominant suppliers rather than squeezing them. Secure multi-year terms and qualify a second source, especially for Aux joyeux ecclésiastiques. Its Côte de Blaye also drove 17% of all growth (Question 1).
  • Find a new Meat/Poultry supplier now. About $94.2K of the last 12 months' sales came from discontinued meat products, and that revenue will be lost once their remaining stock runs out.
  • Consolidate Seafood buying. $99.3K of Seafood sales is spread across eight suppliers, none above 22% of the category. Fewer suppliers would create the volume needed for better terms.

QUESTION 3

Which customers are at risk of leaving, and who should sales call first?

Why it matters

Question 1 showed that growth comes mostly from existing customers ordering more often, so losing an established account is costly. The cheapest time to keep a customer is before they have clearly left. A short, ranked call list lets sales act early.

Approach

  • For each of the 86 customers with at least three orders, found their usual gap between orders (the median), then compared it with the days since their last order. "Today" is 6 May 1998, the last date in the data.
  • At risk = at least 3× the usual gap. Watch = 2–3×. Within each group, customers are ranked by revenue in the last 12 months.
  • Back-tested the rule by applying it on the first day of each month from July 1997 to January 1998 (447 customer snapshots) and checking who ordered within the next 120 days.
  • Excluded three customers with fewer than three orders, which isn't enough to know their rhythm, and two customers who have never ordered.

Main finding

Five customers are at risk ($28.5K in the last 12 months, 3.3% of revenue), and four more are on watch. The top priority is Mère Paillarde (Canada). It usually orders every 18 days but hasn't ordered for 188 days, after buying $12.9K in the last 12 months.

Four of the nine flagged customers are in France, which is consistent with France's flat revenue in Question 1. Their combined Jan–Apr revenue fell from $6.4K in 1997 to $5.3K in 1998.

The back-test supports the at-risk flag, but it's a warning, not a verdict: 72% of customers flagged at risk still ordered within 120 days, against 85% of on-track customers. So the flag nearly doubles the chance a customer doesn't come back (28% vs 15%). Customers at 2–3× their usual gap reordered about as often as on-track ones (88%), so the watch tier is lower priority. We also checked whether customers who deal with many different salespeople are more likely to be overdue, and found no meaningful link (correlation 0.14, not statistically significant).

Business implication

  • Call the five at-risk customers this week, in revenue order, starting with Mère Paillarde, Seven Seas Imports and Victuailles en stock. The five together bought $28.5K in the last 12 months.
  • Match the retention offer to the account. In the back-test, about 72% of customers flagged at risk ordered again within 120 days anyway, so start with a call or a reorder reminder. For valuable accounts that are far overdue or don't respond, a targeted discount to win them back may be worth it. Mère Paillarde, at 10.7× its usual gap, is the clearest case.
  • Look at France as a market, not just individual accounts. Four of the nine flagged customers are French.
  • Run this list every month. The rule uses only order dates, so it's cheap to refresh as new orders arrive, and it catches accounts drifting away before they're gone.

QUESTION 4

Which shipper should get more of our volume, weighing speed against cost?

Why it matters

Every order depends on a carrier, and freight cost $64.9K over the period. If one carrier is reliably faster at a similar cost, shifting volume improves the customer experience. Being able to move volume is also a negotiating lever with the others.

Approach

  • Took the 809 orders that have shipped and measured how many days each carrier took from order to shipment: the typical time, the time by which 90% of orders had shipped, and the share that took over two weeks.
  • Checked with standard statistical tests that the gaps aren't just luck. Then made a like-for-like comparison of orders of similar size, going to the same country, in the same quarter, so a carrier can't look faster just because it gets easier routes.
  • Compared freight cost the same like-for-like way: orders of the same value going to the same country.
  • Limitation: the data records when an order shipped, not when it arrived.

Main finding

Federal Shipping is fastest where it matters, in the slow tail. 90% of its orders shipped within 12 days, against 18 for Speedy Express and 22 for United Package. Only 7.6% of Federal's orders took more than two weeks, compared with 14.9% for United. In a like-for-like comparison (same destination country, similar order size, same quarter), Federal ships 1.7 days sooner than United on average and is 6.9 percentage points less likely to take over two weeks. Both gaps are too large to be explained by chance.

Late rates against the customer's required date are similar across carriers (3.6%–5.1%, a difference small enough to be chance). Meanwhile United, the slowest in the tail, carries the largest and growing share of orders: 37% in late 1996, rising to 43% in 1998.

On cost, Federal is probably a little more expensive. For the same orders, every $100 of freight with United would cost about $116 with Federal. The data can't pin this down exactly: the likely range is $98 to $138. So faster shipping probably comes at a modest premium, which makes this a real trade-off.

Business implication

  • Move time-sensitive and high-value orders from United to Federal. Even moving all 209 United orders from the last 12 months would add only about $3.3K in freight (up to $7.6K at the top of the likely range). That's about 0.4% of revenue, and it would avoid roughly 14 orders a year taking over two weeks.
  • Use the volume as leverage. Before moving it, ask United to commit to a two-week shipping standard or lower rates. Its share is growing, so Northwind has more volume to bargain with each year.
  • The switch is low-friction. Customers already receive orders from a median of 3 different carriers, so changing the mix needs no customer-facing change.
  • Start recording delivery dates. This analysis measures time to shipment, which may partly reflect warehouse and pickup timing. Delivery dates would show the carriers' true transit performance.

DATA & METHODS

How the numbers were produced

  • Data: the course's Northwind workbook: 830 orders, 2,155 order lines, 91 customers, 77 products, 29 suppliers, 9 employees and 3 shippers. Orders run from 4 July 1996 to 6 May 1998.
  • Revenue = unit price × quantity × (1 − discount), using the price on each order line, which differs from the current list price on 31% of lines. Discounts were rounded to two decimals to remove floating-point noise.
  • Cleaning: six columns left in the Product sheet by an earlier exercise were dropped (Cheap, NewPrice, Cheap.1, IsCheap, Unnamed: 14, NewPrice.1). No rows were removed.
  • Time windows: growth compares Jan–Apr 1997 with Jan–Apr 1998. Supplier volume uses May 1997–Apr 1998. Customer recency is measured as of 6 May 1998.
  • Limitations: there's no purchase-cost data, so profit can't be measured and sales stand in for purchase volume. Inventory is a single snapshot. Shipping is measured to the shipment date, not delivery. About 22 months of history is too short to estimate seasonality.
  • Reproduce: all figures, tables and numbers on this page are generated by the Python code at https://github.com/armct477/northwind-analysis. Run python run_analysis.py.
